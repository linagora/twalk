# Revoked consent reduces publication instead of silencing it

When a contact's consent is `revoked`, the Sensor still publishes an inbound event, but without the message body: for a revoked sender the contract no longer requires a body and forbids one, along with the reply and reaction excerpts and an attachment's reference, decryption material and caption (#58) — so a producer that keeps publishing content is invalid, not merely impolite. The event keeps its identity, network, timestamps and consent label. `pending` is unchanged — the Sensor labels, and consumers refuse. Revocation applies to the future only: events already on the bus stay until retention expires, and erasing a contact's history is a separate, explicit action, out of v0.1.

Rationale: the project's promise is that no message leaves the user's infrastructure without consent, so keeping full bodies on the bus for a contact the user just revoked contradicts it, whatever downstream consumers do. Publishing nothing at all goes too far the other way: the user loses the evidence that a message arrived, and a broken bridge becomes indistinguishable from a silent contact. A reduced event keeps the audit trail honest while collecting nothing. Conflating revocation with erasure was rejected in both directions — it surprises the user who only wanted to stop, and deceives the one who believed they had erased.

**Amendment, 2026-09-24 ([#354](https://github.com/linagora/twalk/issues/354), [#351](https://github.com/linagora/twalk/issues/351)): a field of the owner's own data may travel on the owner's own decision, and the calendar's location is the first.**

Reduction was written about a **contact's** words: what a revoked contact wrote does not leave, and the event that says they wrote is reduced rather than suppressed. The collector's calendar half (#280) then applied the same instinct to fields nobody had decided about — the description, the location and the attachments of the owner's own meetings — and withheld all three, always. That was the right default and the wrong permanent answer: the owner asked for the location, because an assistant that knows a meeting is in Paris can say when to leave and one that does not cannot.

So this ADR gains a distinction it did not have. A field is judged by **whose words it is** and by **what deciding about it costs**, not by which event it rides on:

- a **location** is short, is almost always a place, and is what the owner asked for. It travels when the owner has said it may, as a decision recorded like the disclosure's (ADR 0019, ADR 0031) — global, dated, attributed, and **off as a deployment ships**, because the default belongs to the person who has not read the settings screen yet. A location can be a home address; on an invitation it was written by whoever organised the meeting; and it reaches whatever model the deployment named. None of that is a reason to forbid it, and all of it is a reason the owner decides rather than the project.
- a **description** does not travel, at any setting. It is a free-text box written by whoever created the meeting, which on an invitation is a third party who decided nothing here; it carries other people's words, agendas, credentials, and things nobody thought about. What an agent needs of it — is there a video link, is there an agenda at all — it asks for as **facts**, not as text ([#355](https://github.com/linagora/twalk/issues/355)), which is the same move [#281](https://github.com/linagora/twalk/issues/281) made for the agenda itself: widen the questions, not the event.
- **attachments** do not travel either, and neither does an attachment's name. Their existence is a fact; their contents are somebody's file.

The rule the three share: **the owner may widen what leaves about their own data, and never what leaves about someone else's.** A contact's consent is still the only thing that governs a contact's words, and this amendment does not touch it — a withheld organizer takes the location of their invitation with them exactly as they take its title.

Two things follow that are worth stating because they are easy to get wrong. The collector **never reads** the description or the attachments at all, which is stronger than not publishing them: what a process does not hold it cannot leak by a later mistake. And a decision the collector cannot read is **not permission**: a deployment with no Gateway configured, or one whose first read of the switch fails, publishes no location and says so.
