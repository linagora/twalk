# Observing a foreign homeserver is a device of the user's own account

The bring-your-own-account channel (ADR 0009) is served in two stages. **In v0.1 it observes only an account on the homeserver the deployment itself drives**, which is the case of someone who already runs a Synapse and installs Twalk in front of it; the screen says so before asking for credentials, rather than after the user has chosen rooms. **Afterwards, an account on any other homeserver is observed by a device of that account**, created by the Companion during onboarding and holding a session on the user's own homeserver — not by inviting `@sensor:<deployment>` into rooms it would have to federate to reach. The device's access token goes straight into the Sensor's own store and never through the Companion Gateway, so ADR 0011's rule that the Gateway holds no Matrix access token stands unchanged. The device is cross-signed like any other of the user's devices, appears in their device list, and is revocable from any Matrix client without Twalk's involvement. The Sensor therefore becomes one observer per connected Matrix account **beside** its existing single identity, which continues to serve the bridges' portal rooms.

Rationale: the invitation model is not merely unimplemented for a foreign homeserver, it cannot work on the deployments Twalk targets. `@sensor:<deployment>` must *join* a room hosted elsewhere, which needs federation in both directions — a public name, a certificate, a server the other side will talk to — and a deployment reached on loopback through a tunnel is federatable by nobody. Found live against a real corporate account: `.well-known` delegation, SSO through the organisation's identity provider, the redirect back to a tunnelled origin and the room listing all worked, and only the last step could not land ([#138](https://github.com/linagora/twalk/issues/138)). Federating the deployment was rejected as the general answer because it makes a public DNS name and a certificate a precondition of a feature, and because it is the most exposed shape: every colleague in the room sees an unfamiliar account on an unknown server join their conversation, which is exactly the third-party problem [#122](https://github.com/linagora/twalk/issues/122) is about. An appservice on the foreign side was rejected as needing administrative rights on somebody else's homeserver. A device of the user's own account asks nothing of the world — no federation, no invitation, no third party in anybody's room — and it is what the screen's own promise describes: watching over the account you already have is a device of that account, not a stranger admitted to it. It is also the better posture for data protection, since the reader of those messages is the user's own identity rather than a new party.

The costs are real and are accepted rather than argued away. The Sensor stops being one identity with one sync loop and one store, which is a change of shape and not a configuration flag; two observation mechanisms will coexist, because the bridges' portals are local and need no device. A long-lived token must exist somewhere, and putting it in the Sensor's store rather than the Gateway respects ADR 0011's letter while still creating a credential at rest that a compromised host would yield. Encryption is the hard part: a new device reads encrypted history only once cross-signed, an unverified one raises warnings in other people's clients, and the verification runs against a homeserver the deployment does not control. And the device is visible to the user's homeserver administrator — a transparency worth having and worth telling the user about. [#105](https://github.com/linagora/twalk/issues/105), where nothing invites the Sensor into a bridge's portal rooms either, is the same seam from the local side and is decided with this one, so the project grows one observation story rather than two that drifted apart.
